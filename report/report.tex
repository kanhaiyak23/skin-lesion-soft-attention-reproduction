\begin{abstract}
We reproduce Datta \emph{et al.}'s ``Soft-Attention Improves Skin Cancer Classification Performance'' (iMIMIC @ MICCAI 2021) on the seven-class HAM10000 benchmark and then test a hypothesis of our own. Porting the authors' code to TensorFlow~2.20, we match their split (9,187 training / 828 test images), augmentation (51,699 images) and parameter counts exactly. The Inception-ResNet-v2 (IRv2) baseline reproduces within 0.01 of the paper on accuracy, weighted precision and weighted AUC, but the claimed +3.2-point gain from Soft-Attention shrinks to +0.4 points, inside the bootstrap interval of either model. The reproduction exposes the baseline's real weakness: at 91.6\% accuracy it finds only 15 of 34 test melanomas. We pre-registered two hypotheses: (H1) focal loss ($\gamma=2$) as a single change raises melanoma and macro recall; (H2) lowering the melanoma decision threshold, with no retraining, achieves the same. Six training runs (cross-entropy and focal loss, three seeds each) show that focal loss finds more melanomas (25.7 vs.\ 20.3 of 34; pooled McNemar $p=0.011$) only by predicting melanoma two to four times as often: accuracy falls from 0.922 to 0.813, macro recall from 0.755 to 0.684 and melanoma AUC from 0.958 to 0.927. H1 is therefore not supported. H2 is supported on every seed, and a cross-fitted threshold on the cross-entropy model finds more melanomas than focal loss at every matched specificity (29.3 vs.\ 25.7 at specificity 0.90). Run-to-run variance is large (16--26 melanomas across seeds for an identical configuration), which also puts the original attention claim within noise. No clinical claims are made.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Skin cancer is among the most commonly diagnosed cancers worldwide. Melanoma accounts for a small fraction of cases but for most skin-cancer deaths, and survival depends strongly on early detection~\cite{esteva2017}. Dermoscopy improves diagnostic accuracy, but its interpretation is subjective: in a reader study, 58 dermatologists reached a mean melanoma sensitivity of 86.6\% at a specificity of 71.3\%~\cite{haenssle2018}. This has motivated a decade of work on automated classification, beginning with the demonstration that an ImageNet-pretrained convolutional neural network (CNN) can match dermatologists on binary tasks~\cite{esteva2017}.

For multi-class classification, HAM10000~\cite{tschandl2018} is the standard public benchmark. Two of its properties make headline numbers hard to interpret. It is severely imbalanced (nevi are 66.9\% of images, dermatofibroma about 1\%), so accuracy-weighted metrics hide minority-class failures; and its 10,015 images show only 7,470 lesions, so splits that ignore lesion identity leak near-duplicates~\cite{abhishek2025}. Several cited results do not survive these checks~\cite{shetty2022}, and one has been retracted~\cite{densenet2}.

We therefore chose to \emph{reproduce} a published result before proposing a change. Our base paper, Datta \emph{et al.}~\cite{datta2021}, inserts a Soft-Attention module into ImageNet backbones and reports that IRv2 with Soft-Attention reaches 93.8\% weighted precision, 3.2 points above the same backbone without attention. We then used what the reproduction revealed to formulate, pre-register and test a hypothesis of our own.

The project ran in four milestones: a literature review and paper selection (M1), the reproduction (M2), two pre-registered hypotheses (M3), and a multi-seed test of them (M4). Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a verified reproduction of~\cite{datta2021}, with exact agreement in split, augmented-set size and parameter counts, and an itemised list of porting changes;
  \item a gap analysis showing that the claimed Soft-Attention gain cannot be distinguished from noise under the original protocol;
  \item two hypotheses, focal loss (H1) and a melanoma threshold control (H2), stated with direction, magnitude, mechanism and falsification criteria before any experiment;
  \item a six-run, three-seed test with paired statistics, which rejects H1, supports H2, and gives the first measurement of run-to-run variance on this pipeline;
  \item all code, per-run predictions and analysis scripts, so every number here can be regenerated.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Clinical benchmarks}
Esteva \emph{et al.}~\cite{esteva2017} trained a single Inception-v3 on 129,450 clinical images and matched 21 dermatologists on biopsy-proven binary tasks. Haenssle \emph{et al.}~\cite{haenssle2018} supply the human operating point (sensitivity 86.6\%, specificity 71.3\%), which we use as a reference. Tschandl \emph{et al.}~\cite{tschandl2020} showed that AI-assisted clinicians outperform either alone, framing such systems as decision support.

\subsection{Datasets and protocols}
HAM10000~\cite{tschandl2018} documents its multi-source acquisition and the presence of multiple images per lesion. The ISIC 2017 challenge~\cite{codella2018} standardised fixed partitions and balanced accuracy; the ISIC 2018 Task~3 training set \emph{is} HAM10000.

\subsection{Transfer-learning baselines}
Chaturvedi \emph{et al.}~\cite{chaturvedi2020} fine-tuned MobileNet (83.1\% accuracy). Ali \emph{et al.}~\cite{ali2021} fine-tuned EfficientNet B0--B7; B4 was best (87.91\%) and larger models did not improve on it, suggesting that capacity is not the bottleneck on HAM10000.

\subsection{Attention, imbalance and augmentation}
Datta \emph{et al.}~\cite{datta2021} add Soft-Attention to five backbones; IRv2+SA is best. Yao \emph{et al.}~\cite{yao2022} combine RandAugment, DropBlock and class-balanced losses in a single regularised model. Perez \emph{et al.}~\cite{perez2018} ablated 13 augmentation scenarios, our template for one-variable ablation. Lin \emph{et al.}~\cite{lin2017focal} proposed focal loss to down-weight easy examples, the mechanism behind H1. Gessert \emph{et al.}~\cite{gessert2020} won ISIC 2019 with a multi-resolution EfficientNet ensemble.

\subsection{Data quality and integrity}
Abhishek \emph{et al.}~\cite{abhishek2025} documented duplicates, cross-split leakage and label noise in HAM10000 derivatives. Shetty \emph{et al.}~\cite{shetty2022} report 95.18\% on a test set built with augmentation before the split. ``DenseNet-II'' has been retracted~\cite{densenet2}. Rehman \emph{et al.}~\cite{rehman2022} and the review of Naseri and Safaei~\cite{naseri2025} confirm that CNN transfer learning remains the dominant paradigm.

\subsection{Metrics}
Following the paper we report accuracy, weighted precision and weighted one-vs-rest (OvR) AUC. Because nv is 80\% of the test set, we add macro recall, per-class sensitivity, specificity and AUC, 95\% bootstrap intervals (1,000 resamples) and 95\% Wilson intervals for per-class recall. Melanoma recall is the primary clinical metric. In M4 we compare models trained on the same images with the exact McNemar test, which counts the images that only one of the two models gets right, and we compare melanoma recall at \emph{matched specificity}, so that a model is not rewarded merely for predicting melanoma more often.

\section{Reproduced Method (M2)}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Split and augmentation}
Following the official code, lesions with exactly one image form the test pool; a stratified 15\% of that pool gives the 828-image test set, and the remaining 9,187 images form the training set, so no lesion appears on both sides. The authors call \texttt{train\_test\_split} without a seed; we fix \texttt{SEED=42} (in M4 the run seed also sets the split, so each seed has its own test set with identical class counts). Each training class is augmented offline (rotation $180^\circ$, shift 0.1, zoom 0.1, flips) to $\approx$8,000 images, giving 51,699 training images (Fig.~\ref{fig:aug}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{augmented_samples.png}
\caption{Offline-augmented training samples.}
\label{fig:aug}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Models}
\textbf{Backbone.} ImageNet Inception-ResNet-v2 at $299\times299$, truncated where the authors truncate it (an $8\times8\times192$ map inside \texttt{block8\_9}); all layers trainable.

\textbf{Baseline head.} ReLU $\rightarrow$ Dropout(0.5) $\rightarrow$ Flatten $\rightarrow$ Dense(7, softmax); 47,550,631 parameters.

\textbf{Soft-Attention head.} A 16-head Soft-Attention layer computes attention maps by a 3-D convolution over the feature tensor, softmax-normalised over space; attention-weighted and original features are max-pooled, concatenated and passed to the same classifier; 47,535,287 parameters. Following the code rather than the paper text, there is no learnable scalar $\gamma$.

\subsection{Training}
Adam (lr $=0.01$, $\epsilon=0.1$), categorical cross-entropy, class weight 5.0 on mel (in the code, not the paper), batch 16, at most 150 epochs, \texttt{ModelCheckpoint} on best validation accuracy and \texttt{EarlyStopping} on validation loss (patience 30). Under the paper's protocol the validation set \emph{is} the test set; we reproduce this faithfully and discuss it in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Gap and Failure Analysis (M2)}\label{sec:gap}
\subsection{Why our numbers differ from the paper}
\textbf{(1) A different random test set.} The authors' split is unseeded, so every execution draws different test lesions; this is likely the largest effect. \textbf{(2) Tiny minority classes.} One image moves df recall by 16.7 points; all our per-class Wilson intervals overlap the paper's. \textbf{(3) Noisy training with test-set checkpointing.} Adam at lr $=0.01$ makes validation accuracy jump several points between epochs (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}), and the checkpoint is the epoch with the best accuracy on an 80\%-nv test set. \textbf{(4) A different operating point, not a worse model.} AUC, which is threshold-free, matches the paper closely; what differs is where the arg-max decision lands. \textbf{(5) Software and hardware} (Keras 3 kernels, OpenCV augmentation, two-GPU BatchNorm for IRv2+SA), all small.

\subsection{Why the Soft-Attention gain did not reproduce}
The paper's +3.2 points compares one run of each model on two different unseeded test sets. Our runs differ by +0.4 points with overlapping intervals. Soft-Attention did raise melanoma recall from 15 to 21 of 34, but the bootstrap intervals overlap (0.27--0.62 vs.\ 0.44--0.78), and with one run per model the run-to-run variance was unknown at this stage. Section~\ref{sec:m4res} measures it.

\subsection{Failure modes of the baseline}
The most frequent errors are the same for both models: bkl$\rightarrow$nv (19 / 19), mel$\rightarrow$nv (9 / 9), akiec$\rightarrow$nv (4 / 8) and mel$\leftrightarrow$bkl (13 / 7). Minority lesions are absorbed into nv (Fig.~\ref{fig:cm}), often with $p\approx1.00$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}). Melanoma ranking is reasonable (AUC 0.915), but recall at the arg-max is only 0.44 at a specificity of 0.982: the model separates melanoma well but rarely \emph{predicts} it. This observation motivates both hypotheses below.

\section{Hypotheses (M3)}\label{sec:hyp}
Both hypotheses were written, with their falsification criteria, before any M4 experiment. They are applied to the IRv2 baseline \emph{without} Soft-Attention, because attention gave no measurable gain.

\subsection{H1: focal loss}
\textbf{Motivation.} The training set is already balanced by oversampling, so class frequency is not the remaining problem. About 98\% of training examples end up correctly classified; their many small cross-entropy gradients drown out the few hard, confusable examples (mel/bkl vs.\ nv).

\textbf{Hypothesis.} \emph{Replacing categorical cross-entropy with focal loss ($\gamma=2$)~\cite{lin2017focal} will increase melanoma recall and macro recall, because focal loss down-weights well-classified examples and concentrates the gradient on hard lesions.} Focal loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FL}(p_t) = -\alpha\,(1-p_t)^{\gamma}\log(p_t),
\end{equation}
which equals cross-entropy at $\gamma=0$. We set $\alpha=1$ so that no class re-weighting is added on top of the existing $\times5$ melanoma weight. Every other setting is unchanged.

\textbf{Mechanism.} An example predicted correctly at $p=0.9$ contributes $-\log 0.9\approx0.105$ under cross-entropy but $0.01\times0.105\approx0.001$ under focal loss, 100$\times$ less; a hard example at $p=0.3$ keeps about half its loss. We expected the decision boundary to move away from nv without better ranking, so AUC should stay flat.

\textbf{Predictions} (mean over seeds): melanoma recall 0.44 $\rightarrow$ 0.50--0.60; macro recall 0.72 $\rightarrow$ 0.74--0.77; weighted precision 0.89--0.91; accuracy down by at most 0.01; weighted AUC unchanged ($\pm0.005$).

\textbf{Falsification.} Reject if mean melanoma recall does not exceed the baseline mean by more than one standard deviation, or if macro recall does not improve (or improves only through df/vasc). Partial support at best if weighted precision falls by more than 0.02, since a threshold change could produce the same trade-off.

\textbf{Paired decision rule} (fixed before M4). Within a seed, CE and focal are scored on the same 828 images and compared with an exact McNemar test; melanoma discordant pairs are pooled over seeds (102 melanomas). H1 is \emph{supported} only if the one-SD rule holds, focal finds more melanomas on \emph{every} seed, and the pooled test gives $p<0.05$; \emph{rejected} if focal finds no more melanomas on average; otherwise \emph{inconclusive}.

\subsection{H2: the melanoma threshold control}
\textbf{Hypothesis.} \emph{Lowering the melanoma decision threshold on the trained cross-entropy model, with no retraining, will raise melanoma recall to at least 20/34 while keeping melanoma specificity $\geq0.95$, because the baseline's errors come from the arg-max operating point, not from poor features.} The decision rule predicts mel if $p(\text{mel})\geq t$ and otherwise the arg-max over the other six classes; $t$ is the only variable.

\textbf{Protocol.} No threshold is chosen on the images it scores. We use stratified 5-fold cross-fitting on the test set: for each fold, $t$ is the lowest threshold with specificity $\geq$ the target on the other four folds, then applied to the held-out fold. The procedure is repeated over 20 random fold assignments. Operating points: specificity $\geq0.95$, $\geq0.90$ and 0.713 (the dermatologists' mean~\cite{haenssle2018}).

\textbf{Falsification.} Reject if cross-fitted recall at specificity $\geq0.95$ is below 20/34 or weighted precision drops by more than 0.02; inconclusive if the chosen threshold varies by more than 0.2 across folds.

\textbf{Why H2 matters for H1.} If a free threshold change raises recall, arg-max recall alone cannot show that a new loss helps; H1 must then also be judged at matched specificity.

\section{Experimental Setup (M4)}\label{sec:m4setup}
We trained six models: CE and focal loss, each with seeds 42, 7 and 2024. A single script (\texttt{train.py}, generated from the reproduction notebook) takes the loss and seed as arguments; everything else is as in Section~\ref{sec:method}. Each run used one NVIDIA A40 on RunPod (TF~2.20). The pre-registration named an RTX 4090; none was available, so all six runs used the same A40 type instead, 17.6 GPU-hours in total. A shell script (\texttt{run\_pod.sh}) installs dependencies, downloads HAM10000, trains, and uploads logs and predictions to a private dataset every ten minutes. A separate script (\texttt{M4\_analyze.py}) computes every table and verdict below from the saved test-set probabilities.

\subsection{Run-to-run variance}
With identical code and hardware, CE found 16, 26 or 19 melanomas depending only on the seed (standard deviation 5.1). The seed sets both the training randomness and which single-image lesions form the 828-image test set: the three test sets have the same class counts but share only 12 images. CE and focal loss always share the test set of their seed, so the paired comparisons above are unaffected, and its macro recall ranged from 0.722 to 0.808. CE seed 42 on the A40 found 16/34, close to the 15/34 of the M2 Kaggle run.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}
\textbf{Focal loss changes the operating point, not the model's discrimination.} Focal loss raised melanoma detections exactly as a lower threshold would, but on a model that ranks melanoma \emph{worse} (AUC 0.927 vs.\ 0.958). It is therefore dominated by the cheaper alternative: the cross-entropy model plus a threshold. This is the scenario anticipated by the ``partial support'' clause in H1, and it is the reason we pre-registered H2.

\textbf{Why focal loss underperformed.} Two factors are likely. First, the training set is balanced by oversampling and melanoma already carries a $\times5$ class weight, so focal loss's down-weighting of easy nv examples adds a second, strong push towards melanoma; the model over-shoots. Second, the paper's optimiser settings (lr $0.01$, $\epsilon=0.1$) were tuned for cross-entropy; with smaller focal-loss values the effective step size and the early-stopping behaviour change, and training became longer and less stable. Tuning these would be a second variable and is left as a separate hypothesis.

\textbf{Implications for the base paper.} The seed alone, through training randomness and the choice of test images, moved CE melanoma recall by 10 images. The M2 difference between IRv2 and IRv2+SA (15 vs.\ 21 melanomas) and the paper's +3.2-point precision gain both come from single runs on single unseeded splits, and are within this variation. Claims of this kind on HAM10000 need several seeds and paired tests.

\textbf{Evaluation practice.} Comparing classifiers at the arg-max rewards whichever model predicts the minority class more often. Melanoma recall should be reported at matched specificity, or alongside AUC, with the threshold chosen on data it is not scored on.

%=====================================================================
\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
Under the paper's protocol the test set is also the validation set: it drives checkpoint selection and early stopping, so every absolute number in this report, the paper's and ours, is optimistically biased; it affects CE and focal equally. The test set has only 34 melanomas (one image is 2.9 recall points), 6 df and 10 vasc images. Three seeds give only a rough standard deviation, which is why the paired test was added in advance. Focal loss was run with optimiser settings tuned for cross-entropy. H2 was pre-registered on the M2 checkpoint, whose probabilities were not saved, and was tested on the three new CE runs; its Soft-Attention arm and the planned clean-validation run were not carried out. M2 used Kaggle T4 GPUs and M4 used A40 GPUs. HAM10000 comes from two centres and a predominantly fair-skinned population, so results may not generalise across skin tones or acquisition settings.

\textbf{No clinical claims are made.} Nothing here is validated for diagnostic use; all metrics are benchmark results on a curated research dataset. Consistent with~\cite{tschandl2020}, the appropriate framing is decision support for a qualified clinician.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We reproduced Datta \emph{et al.}'s pipeline on HAM10000 with an exact match in split, augmentation and model size. The IRv2 baseline reproduces within 0.01 on the paper's headline metrics, but the reported 3.2-point gain from Soft-Attention shrinks to 0.4 points. The reproduction showed that the baseline's weakness is melanoma recall at the arg-max decision, not melanoma discrimination. Our pre-registered hypothesis that focal loss would fix this was not supported: over three seeds it finds more melanomas only by predicting melanoma far more often, at a large cost in accuracy, macro recall and AUC. A cross-fitted threshold on the cross-entropy model, which needs no retraining, does better at every matched specificity. Run-to-run variance is large enough to explain the original attention gain.

Future work: (i) evaluate every model at matched specificity across several seeds, including IRv2+SA; (ii) re-test focal loss with its own learning rate and a clean, lesion-grouped validation split, as a new pre-registered hypothesis; (iii) separate validation from test to remove the optimistic bias of the original protocol.
